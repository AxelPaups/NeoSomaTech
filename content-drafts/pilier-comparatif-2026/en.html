Introduction: finding the right exoskeleton among dozens of models

Skiing, hiking, a fragile knee, back pain at work, lifting loads in a warehouse... the word "exoskeleton" now covers very different products, priced anywhere from $200 to over $4,000. Without a clear starting point, it's easy to get lost.

This guide has one goal: help you identify the product family that matches your use case, then point you to the detailed comparison and the relevant product pages. If you already know which model you want, head to our interactive comparison tool, which shows full technical specs.

 

Chapter 1: the 4 main families of exoskeletons

1. Knee exoskeletons for sport (skiing, hiking)

Mechanical (spring-based) or textile devices that offload the knee during effort. This is the most mature category for consumers, with products like the Ski-Mojo or the Stoko K1 tights.

2. Motorized walking exoskeletons (hiking, seniors, light rehab)

Active systems (motor + battery) at the hip that assist walking uphill and downhill alike. This is the newest and most technological segment, led by brands like Hypershell, Dnsys and VIGX.

3. Back-support exoskeletons (physical jobs)

Built for jobs that require bending, lifting or working with raised arms all day (construction, logistics, caregiving). The Hapo range covers the back, shoulders and neck depending on the movement involved.

4. Active vs passive biomechanical support

A technical distinction that cuts across all the families above: a passive exoskeleton (springs, textile) uses no power and costs less; an active exoskeleton (motor, sensors, battery) provides stronger assistance but needs more maintenance and a bigger budget. Chapter 3: how much does an exoskeleton cost in 2026?

Prices vary a lot depending on the technology and use case: a passive textile support starts around $200, a spring-based model like the Ski-Mojo sits around $750, and motorized walking models range from $700 to over $2,000 depending on battery life and materials. Professional back-support exoskeletons, being more specialized, range from $700 to over $4,500 depending on the body part supported and the job targeted.

 

Chapter 4: how to choose the right exoskeleton

  • Your actual use case: occasional sport, daily activity, and a repetitive professional constraint don't call for the same product.

  • Active or passive: a passive model is often enough for sport or light support; an active one is worth it for stronger assistance or extended activity.

  • Your budget: a well-matched $300 product beats a high-end $2,000 model used the wrong way.

  • Medical context: if you have a condition (arthritis, recent surgery, disc herniation...), ask a healthcare professional before buying — this guide does not replace medical advice.

 

Frequently asked questions

What is the best exoskeleton in 2026?

There's no single "best" exoskeleton: the right choice depends on your use case (sport, walking, work) and your budget. Use the table above to find your category, then check the matching detailed guide.

Is an active exoskeleton always better than a passive one?

No. A passive model is lighter, easier to maintain and cheaper; an active model provides stronger assistance but costs more and needs battery charging. The best choice depends on how much assistance you actually need.

Can you try an exoskeleton before buying?

Some ski models are available for daily rental at select resorts. For professional or motorized walking models, check directly with the manufacturer.

Are consumer exoskeletons covered by insurance?

As of now, most sport or comfort exoskeletons are not covered by standard health insurance. Some prescribed medical devices may be partially reimbursed: always check this with your doctor or insurer.
